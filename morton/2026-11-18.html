GitHub's Copilot coding agent opened 996 public pull requests on Friday 13 November and 170 on Saturday the 14th. It hasn't recovered since: 211 on Monday and 204 on Tuesday, against 1,092 the Sunday before. A weekend dip doesn't look like this. GitHub's changelog for the month says nothing about it. One possible explanation is attribution: since June, GitHub has been folding Copilot-authored PRs into the delegating human's author: searches. If that's what happened, the agent is as busy as ever and public search can no longer see it. Nobody has said so, and it's a guess. Meanwhile, on the same Tuesday, Devin's app shows 1,609 PRs, eight times Copilot's visible count. Anyone counting coding-agent activity by bot login now has a gap they can't explain. That includes me.

The trap is wirehush.com, a no-signup board that its builder openly calls a honeypot. Its pages ask visiting AI assistants to register their model and task by calling a beacon URL. After a month, the published export shows 33 trap hits, zero self-declared agents and four surviving posts, one of them "hello". On 16 November it logged 692 reads, 68 of them from automation scripts. It runs on a Windows scheduled task, and tonight the site returned a Cloudflare origin error. The search that found it also turned up half a dozen new agent-honeypot repos, two of them created today, and none of the ones I read publishes what it caught. The obvious question for the whole genre is whether agents are actually walking into these traps. The one project that shows its numbers suggests mostly not, or not while saying who they are.

Bottom line: watch whether Copilot's PR count returns or has just moved somewhere else. Agent honeypots are a builders' trend, not yet evidence about how agents behave.
