The global layer: how the FSB's AI work reaches EU and UK supervisors

On 15 October 2026 the Financial Stability Board, which coordinates G20 financial regulators, finalised 12 sound practices for AI in financial institutions. They are explicitly not binding. This page traces the layer above a firm's own regulator, from the FSB to the EU's systemic-risk board and supervisors and to the Bank of England and the FCA, and asks one question: does any of it change what a firm must actually do?

Confidence key: confirmed primary source checked directly · likely credible secondary source(s), primary not yet confirmed · unverified single-sourced or unconfirmed, included with an explicit caveat, never as settled fact.

Short answer: almost nothing here is new law. The FSB sets expectations, not rules. The EU and UK authorities route frontier-AI risk through frameworks firms already have (DORA in the EU; the Consumer Duty, the senior-managers regime and operational-resilience rules in the UK). The one hard date in this picture is the ECB's 31 October 2026 deadline for the euro area's directly supervised banks. What the layer above changes is where supervisors look, and the vocabulary they use when they get there.

1. The standard-setter: the FSB's final sound practices confirmed

The FSB consulted on a "menu" of 12 sound practices on 10 June 2026 and published the final report on 15 October 2026. The 12 practices sit in two groups: organisation-wide governance (strategic direction and oversight; governance and accountability; building AI risks into the risk-management framework, with documentation; organisational adaptability) and lifecycle management (materiality and risk assessment; selection process; data governance; explainability and transparency; performance management; human oversight; cyber and ICT risk management; third-party AI risk management). The report's executive summary says: "The sound practices are non-prescriptive and do not constitute a binding international standard." Its introduction says sound practices "specific to agentic AI and multi-agent systems remain nascent" (FSB, final report, 15 October 2026, PDF accessed 2026-10-15 to 2026-10-17).

The FSB's separate overview of consultation responses, published the same day, is more candid about how the report was finished. About 160 responses came in. New material on agentic AI was added anyway: "These additional considerations on agentic AI are not fully consulted on". And on the systemic question: "no changes have been made to address broader systemic risks or macroprudential concerns arising from AI use" (FSB, overview of consultation responses, 15 October 2026, accessed 2026-10-15 and re-checked 2026-10-25).

Why it would be true: the FSB has no powers over firms. It issues guidance that national supervisors then cite, so a non-binding menu is its normal output. Its macroprudential work runs separately, in its vulnerabilities assessments, so leaving systemic questions out of a firm-level menu is consistent. Adding agentic-AI material without a second consultation is unusual for a standard-setter. It is a trade of process for speed, and the FSB says so itself.

2. The political hand-off: two letters to the G20 confirmed

The FSB's Chair is Andrew Bailey, who is also Governor of the Bank of England. His letter to G20 finance ministers and central bank governors, printed date 28 August 2026 and posted on 31 August, said: "For the financial system, the most immediate concern is the potential impact of frontier AI on cyber risk." The same letter named "stretched asset valuations (particularly artificial intelligence-related investments)" as a market-correction risk (FSB, August 2026 letter, PDF, accessed 2026-10-11 and 2026-10-13).

His October letter, dated 8 October and posted on 12 October, handed over the practices: "With that in mind, we are submitting to this meeting the FSB's first response on this topic." and "To foster responsible adoption, I urge the G20 to champion these practices." It also left the systemic question open, noting "broader macroprudential issues that may warrant continued consideration and coordination" (FSB, October 2026 letter, PDF, accessed 2026-10-13 and re-checked 2026-10-25).

Reading the two together: the FSB names two AI risks to the financial system, frontier-AI cyber attacks and an AI-valuation correction. The sound practices address neither at the system level. They address how individual firms govern their own AI. That is a design choice, not an oversight, but it means the menu is not the FSB's answer to the risks its own Chair puts first.

3. The EU layer: a systemic warning, then supervisors confirmed

The EU moved earlier and harder than the FSB. On 25 June 2026 the European Systemic Risk Board adopted Warning ESRB/2026/3 on systemic cyber risks from frontier AI models, published on 7 July. It raised its systemic cyber-risk rating from "elevated" to "severe", and ECB Banking Supervision wrote to the chief executives of the banks it supervises directly (ESRB, press release, 7 July 2026, accessed 2026-09-18). Per the warning itself, those banks are to "develop by 31 October 2026 a comprehensive action plan outlining concrete measures" (ESRB/2026/3, 25 June 2026, read via a text proxy, accessed 2026-09-18). On 31 July the three European Supervisory Authorities (EBA, EIOPA, ESMA) followed with a joint statement that cites the warning by its reference number. It sits inside DORA, the EU's existing digital-operational-resilience law, and asks firms and national supervisors to use it "as a basis for supervisory dialogue" (EBA, press release, 31 July 2026, accessed 2026-09-09; statement JC 2026 25 read 2026-09-20). This site's ESRB/ESAs page covers that sequence in detail.

On 1 October, Christine Lagarde, speaking as ESRB Chair, set out three channels by which AI could become systemic: firms using similar models that "assess a shock in much the same way and make similar trades in response"; frontier-AI cyber attacks; and dependence on a few non-European model providers (ECB, "Where AI risks meet", 1 October 2026, accessed 2026-10-03).

The contrast with the FSB: the ESRB treats frontier-AI cyber risk as a systemic question and attached a deadline to it. The FSB menu, by its own account, made no changes for systemic concerns. The two are not in conflict. They are working at different levels, and the binding step, the 31 October action plans, applies only to the euro area's directly supervised banks, not to the wider set of DORA-covered firms such as asset managers.

4. The UK layer: test and supervise, don't legislate first confirmed

The FCA's standing position is "We do not plan to introduce extra regulations for AI." (FCA, "AI and the FCA: our approach", first published 8 September 2025, last updated 2 October 2026, re-checked 2026-10-25). Its two autumn publications both carry the same disclaimer. Its 2 September multi-firm review of frontier AI and cyber resilience "does not introduce new rules, guidance or regulatory expectations" (FCA, 2 September 2026, accessed 2026-09-08). Its 19 October good-practice examples "do not create new requirements" (FCA, first published 19 October 2026, accessed 2026-10-20). See this site's FCA/SEC comparison for the firm-level detail.

At the Bank of England, Bailey wrote on 30 September, in a signed Insights article, "But regulation is not, in my view, the right place to start." and "A sensible starting point is rigorous model testing, conducted before and after deployment." (Bank of England, "Frontier AI and the question of governance", 30 September 2026, accessed 2026-10-17 and re-checked 2026-10-25). The Financial Policy Committee's record of its 25 September meeting, published the same day, says "More capable open-weight models could pose additional risks to financial stability", and that "under permissive or weakened safeguards, increasingly autonomous models could take unexpected actions" (Bank of England, FPC record, published 30 September 2026, accessed 2026-10-18 and re-checked 2026-10-25). The record refers to test-environment incidents in the third quarter of 2026 only in general terms. It names no model, lab or incident.

A caution on linking these: the article and the FPC record came out on the same day, and some press coverage connected them. The Bank itself does not: the record does not mention the article. Treat them as two separately authored statements that happen to agree.
