There is no single official figure. The government does not routinely collect capacity data; its 1.6 GW estimate covers co-location sites only, which is why IT-power methods come out higher (Oxford Economics: 2.9 GW). The government's own preliminary range for 2030 is 3.3–6.3 GW. This page uses the Computer Weekly / Barbour ABI project data for the pipeline so the charts are internally consistent. Summary of all of these in the House of Commons Library, CBP-10315 (27 May 2026).

Demand vs supply — the national picture

NESO's data-centre demand estimate against the theoretical ceiling of the operational fleet (MW × 8,760 h). The demand line sits at an implied fleet-average load factor of roughly 0.4–0.55 — shown as the shaded band. Per-site utilisation is not public anywhere and is not synthesised here. The system-level tension: NESO counted 50 GW of data centres in the connection queue, against 4.9–6.2 GW likely to be built by 2030. Ofgem called the queue "far beyond forecast future need".

Reported vs location-based — the scope-2 gap

Fleet emissions can be counted two ways: location-based (grid average × consumption) or market-based (netting renewable certificates & PPAs). Vendors lead with the second; the difference is what the certificates and contracts net off. The GHG Protocol allows it, but a certificate match is not the same as zero-carbon power at the time it was used. This site stays location-based throughout — handbook §4 explains why.

Scope 2 only, Mt CO₂e, latest reporting year: Microsoft FY2025 (to June 2025) and Google calendar 2025. Both figures are the companies' own, from their 2026 reports. Microsoft's market-based figure rose tenfold in the year after it stopped using unbundled renewable certificates. Context: scope 3 was 86% of Microsoft's FY2025 footprint, and Google's electricity use grew 37% in 2025.
