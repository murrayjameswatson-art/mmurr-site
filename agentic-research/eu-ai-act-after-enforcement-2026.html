The EU AI Act's first enforcement steps: information requests, a cyber plan, and a vendor's agent rules

Within a month of the AI Act's enforcement powers taking effect on 2 August 2026, the AI Office reportedly sent its first formal information requests to more than 30 general-purpose AI providers. Member States' AI Board was briefed on the Commission's cybersecurity-and-AI action plan. And Microsoft rewrote its internal responsible-AI standard around agents, the gap no binding framework yet covers.

This page follows on from The EU AI Act in 2026, which covers the moved high-risk deadlines, the August 2026 transparency rules and the enforcement start date. That page says what the Act requires. The AI Office's first information requests likely

MLex, a specialist regulatory news service, reported on 26 August 2026 that more than 30 AI companies, including Anthropic, OpenAI and Meta Platforms, had received information requests from the EU on safety, security and how they train their models, prompted by "several high-profile hacking incidents" (MLex, 26 August 2026, accessed 2026-09-29; paywalled, headline and lead read).

Agence Europe, a Brussels EU-affairs wire, reported the same action in an article dated 2 September 2026 (Europe Daily Bulletin No. 13929, 3 September). It says the Commission set out the details on Tuesday 1 September. Per that article, Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier described two strands: the safety and security of the most advanced models, and copyright and transparency. Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen's written statement, as reported, addressed only the second strand, which targets providers that had not taken part in informal compliance dialogues with the AI Office (Agence Europe, 2 September 2026, accessed 2026-10-09; the visible text is cut off behind a login prompt and names no recipients).

Why it would be true: requests for information are the AI Act's first-stage tool for general-purpose AI models (Article 91), and the Commission's powers over those providers started on 2 August 2026. Asking questions a few weeks later, after a summer of AI-agent security incidents (see this site's incident-pacing page), is the expected order of events. Requests like these are confidential correspondence, so the lack of a Commission press release is what you would expect. It isn't evidence against the reports.

Why it matters: under Article 101(1)(b) of the AI Act, the Commission may fine a general-purpose AI provider up to 3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher, if it intentionally or negligently "failed to comply with a request for a document or for information pursuant to Article 91" or "supplied incorrect, incomplete or misleading information" (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, published in the Official Journal 12 July 2024; article text read via artificialintelligenceact.eu, accessed 2026-10-10) confirmed. That makes the reply to a request a compliance event in its own right, whatever the Office later decides to investigate.

Why only "likely": two editorially independent specialist outlets agree on the substance, and a named Commission spokesperson is quoted. But no Commission or AI Office page states it, and the date (26 August to 1 September) and the recipients vary from outlet to outlet. A leak followed by an official briefing would explain the spread. That explanation is an inference, not something either outlet says.

The AI Board is briefed on the cybersecurity-and-AI action plan confirmed

The AI Board, the body of Member State representatives that advises the Commission on applying the Act, held its ninth meeting on 17 September 2026 under the Irish Presidency. The agenda covered implementation and enforcement priorities, frontier AI capabilities and recent incidents, uptake of the transparency rules, and market-surveillance coordination. "This AI Board meeting was the first attended by Moldova as an observer," and "The Board members were also updated on the Action Plan on Cybersecurity and AI." The tenth meeting "is expected to take place" on 18 November 2026, alongside the Apply AI Summit in Brussels on 17 November (European Commission, published 18 September 2026, accessed 2026-09-21 and re-fetched 2026-10-09).

The action plan itself is a non-binding Commission communication published on 7 July 2026. It names three deliverables: "The EU Agency for Cybersecurity and the Commission's Joint Research Centre will create a secure platform to test AI for cybersecurity"; "The Commission will work with the EU Agency for Cybersecurity to define a European blueprint for structured access to advanced AI capabilities for cybersecurity"; and "The Commission will also launch the EU Grand Challenge on AI for cybersecurity" (European Commission, 7 July 2026, accessed 2026-09-21). The Commission's own pages give no completion dates for these. Some secondary coverage gives an "end of 2026" date for the testing platform, but I couldn't trace it to Commission wording, so it is unverified and isn't relied on here.

Why it would be true: briefing the Member State coordination body on a Commission initiative ten weeks after it was announced is routine process. The same frontier-model cyber theme is on the EU's financial regulators' agenda in the same months (see this site's ESRB/ESAs page).

A vendor fills part of the agent-governance gap confirmed

The earlier page notes that the main frameworks enterprises lean on (the AI Act, NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001) weren't written with autonomous, tool-using agents in mind. On 1 September 2026 Microsoft published its "third annual Responsible AI Transparency Report", with a blog post by its Chief Responsible AI Officer, Natasha Crampton. The post says: "This year, we re-engineered our Responsible AI Standard to make it more adaptive to evolving technical realities, uses, risks, and regulatory requirements." On agents: "Governing these systems requires us to think beyond the behavior of an individual model or application to interactions among models, agents, applications, tools, data, and people," and "Our work increasingly focuses on controls such as agent identities, tool permissions, and monitoring of actions." It also names a new AI Red Teaming Agent and "an External Red Team Alliance with 18 universities across six continents," and says Microsoft is certified against ISO 42001 for products including Microsoft 365 Copilot, Foundry and GitHub Copilot (Microsoft, 1 September 2026, accessed 2026-09-25 and re-fetched 2026-10-10).

Why it would be true: a vendor shipping agents before any binding external standard exists has a reason to publish its own controls, both to manage a larger failure surface (an agent with tool access can act, not just answer) and to show customers and regulators something concrete. The limit: this is Microsoft's account of its own internal standard. It is not an external audit, and it binds only Microsoft.
